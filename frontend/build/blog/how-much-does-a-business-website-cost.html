How Much Does a Business Website Cost in 2026?

A transparent breakdown of website pricing — what drives the cost, what you should expect, and how to evaluate proposals from agencies and freelancers.

Published July 2026 · 8 min read

One of the most common questions business owners ask is: "How much will my website cost?" The honest answer is that it depends — but not in a vague, unhelpful way. There are specific factors that drive pricing, and understanding them helps you evaluate proposals with confidence.

The short answer

A professional business website in 2026 typically costs between $2,000 and $25,000 for a custom build. Template-based solutions with minimal customization can start lower. Complex e-commerce or web application projects can go higher. The price depends on scope, design complexity, content, and the team you hire.

What drives the cost of a website

1. Number of pages and content depth

A 5-page brochure site costs less than a 20-page site with service pages, case studies, a blog, and multiple landing pages. More pages mean more design, more copywriting, more development, and more testing. If you need help writing the content (not just designing the layout), that adds to the scope.

2. Design complexity

A clean, professional layout using proven patterns is faster to build than a highly custom design with animations, interactive elements, and unique section layouts. Custom illustration or photography also adds cost. Most businesses benefit more from clear messaging than from elaborate design.

3. Functionality and integrations

Contact forms and basic analytics are standard. But if you need appointment booking, payment processing, CRM integration, membership areas, multi-language support, or custom calculators, each feature adds development time. Integration with third-party tools (Stripe, HubSpot, Mailchimp, etc.) requires API work and testing.

4. E-commerce

Adding a product catalog, shopping cart, checkout flow, inventory management, and shipping calculations significantly increases scope. A simple store with 10–50 products is different from a catalog with hundreds of SKUs, variant options, and discount rules.

5. SEO and content strategy

A good website is built with SEO foundations: proper HTML structure, metadata, page speed, mobile responsiveness, sitemap, and crawlable architecture. Going beyond the basics — keyword research, content strategy, blog setup, schema markup — adds planning and implementation time.

6. Who builds it

Freelancers, boutique agencies, and large agencies have different pricing structures. A freelancer might charge $2,000–$8,000. A boutique agency like The Easy Build typically ranges from $3,000–$15,000. Large agencies with overhead, project managers, and multiple specialists can charge $15,000–$50,000+. Price does not always equal quality — what matters is clear communication, transparent milestones, and accountable delivery.

What a good website proposal should include

  • A clear scope of work listing every page, feature, and deliverable
  • A timeline with milestones so you can track progress
  • A breakdown of what is included (design, development, content, SEO basics) and what is not
  • Payment terms tied to milestones, not just upfront payment
  • Post-launch support details — what happens after the site goes live
  • Ownership clarity — who owns the design files, code, and domain

Hidden costs to watch for

  • Hosting and domain: $10–$50/month for most business sites. Some agencies bundle this; others charge separately.
  • Ongoing maintenance: Security updates, plugin updates, content changes, and monitoring. Budget $50–$300/month if outsourcing.
  • Content creation: Professional copywriting, photography, and video are often underestimated. If you are providing your own content, factor in the time it takes to prepare it.
  • Revisions beyond scope: Most proposals include 2–3 rounds of revisions. Major direction changes after design approval can add cost.

Template vs custom: which is right for you?

Template-based sites (WordPress themes, Squarespace, Wix) work well when the business is straightforward, the budget is tight, and the primary goal is to exist online. Custom builds make sense when positioning, trust, conversion optimization, unique functionality, or integration with business systems matter. Many businesses start with a template and graduate to custom when they see the limitations.

How to get started

Before reaching out to a developer or agency, prepare answers to these questions:

  • What is the website for? (Lead generation, sales, information, credibility)
  • Who is the primary audience?
  • What pages do you need?
  • Do you need e-commerce, booking, or other functionality?
  • Do you have content ready, or do you need help creating it?
  • What is your realistic budget range?
  • What is your timeline?

Having these answers makes the discovery call faster and the proposal more accurate.
